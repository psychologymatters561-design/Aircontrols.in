What’s at stake

A hot room is a refund. A hot dining floor is an empty one.

Hospitality is the one sector where the customer is paying, in that moment, for comfort. A guest room at 29°C is a front-desk argument, a refund and a one-star review with the AC in the first sentence. A dining room that loses cooling during Saturday service empties tables you will not refill that night.

And hospitality estates fail differently: kitchen grease loads coils and filters far faster than office dust, and peak stress arrives exactly at peak occupancy — full house, every condenser flat out, into a Delhi summer evening.

How the contract runs in hospitality

FAQ

Specifics for this sector.

Can rooms and floors be serviced without guest disruption?

Yes — room units are serviced with housekeeping’s vacancy schedule, floor by floor, and public-area plant before service hours. We work 7:00 AM to 10:00 PM every day, so the calendar bends around occupancy, not the other way round.

Why do restaurant ACs fail faster than office ACs?

Grease. Kitchen-adjacent evaporator coils and filters load with airborne oil that ordinary dust never matches, and condensers on service lanes clog with exhaust discharge. A restaurant estate on an office-style once-a-year service is a mid-service failure waiting for a Saturday night — dining-area units need shorter cycles.

Our failures always seem to happen on full-occupancy nights. Why?

Because that is when the estate runs at maximum load: every room occupied, every cover seated, condensers working into a 40°C evening. Marginal units — low gas, tired capacitors, dirty coils — carry fine at half load and fail at full. Finding marginal units at half load is precisely what the scheduled visits are for.

Does one contract cover rooms, dining, kitchens and banquet space?

Yes — splits and cassettes in rooms and dining, ducted or VRV/VRF in banquet and lobby space, high-duty kitchen-adjacent units on shorter cycles. One inventory, one report, one number to call at 7 pm on a Saturday.
