AC AMC for offices: how to evaluate a maintenance vendor

The quotes will look identical and the difference surfaces in June. Ten questions to put to any office AC vendor — and for each, the answer that should end the meeting.

Choosing an AC maintenance vendor for an office is a procurement decision that will be judged in June, by people who were not in the meeting where it was made. The quotes will look similar, the brochures will use the same words, and the difference between a managed estate and a summer of complaints lives in details the quotes are built to blur. Here is how to evaluate an office AC AMC vendor with ten questions — each with the answer that should worry you.

1. “Show me a condition report from a current client.”

The single highest-signal question, and it should come first. A vendor doing real maintenance produces per-unit written reports as a by-product of the work — readings, condition grades, action lists — and can show you a redacted one within a day. A vendor who cannot is telling you what their service visits actually contain. (Ours is published: the report format.)

2. “What exactly happens in a service visit? Itemise it.”

“Complete servicing” is not an answer; a numbered checklist is. Look specifically for measurements: refrigerant pressure against manufacturer specification, capacitor microfarads against rating, current draw. A checklist of verbs (cleaned, checked, inspected) with no numbers is a wash-and-wipe service dressed up. The checklist a visit should run is in what’s included in an AC AMC.

3. “What is the response time, in writing, and what happens when it slips?”

The number matters (ours is 2 hours for commercial sites), but the second half of the question matters more. A vendor with a real SLA has an escalation path: who you call when the promised response has not appeared, and what the contract says about it. A vendor whose answer is “that never happens” has never been busy.

4. “Who, by name, owns our account?”

Offices fail vendors slowly — a missed visit here, an unanswered call there — and diffuse responsibility is how it happens. You want a named point of contact who knows your site, plus a dispatch number that answers regardless of one person’s phone. One-man operations fail the second half; call-centre chains fail the first.

5. “How will you schedule around our working day?”

A competent office vendor talks immediately about zones and hours: cabins in rotation during the day, open-floor cassettes and ducted plant before 9 or after close, server-room units on their own tighter cycle. A vendor who plans to service an occupied open floor at 2 pm has not done much office work. Weekend and early-morning capability is a fact to verify, not assume — ask what hours they actually staff. (Air Control works 7 AM–10 PM, seven days.)

6. “What does the quote cover, per unit, and what is excluded?”

Insist on the per-unit inventory basis and the tier being explicit — non-comprehensive, semi-comprehensive or comprehensive — with the exclusions listed specifically. The cheapest quote in the stack is usually cheapest because of what it quietly does not include, and you will discover the difference in June, at emergency rates. How honest per-unit pricing is built is covered in the pricing guide.

7. “Can you handle everything we run — and everything we might?”

Inventory your estate before the meeting: splits, cassettes, ducted plant, VRV/VRF, the server-room units, the odd window unit in the store. A vendor competent on splits but improvising on VRV/VRF will learn on your plant. Ask which brands and system types they service and whether one contract covers the mixed estate.

8. “What paper does procurement get?”

GST invoicing, written scope-of-work, the service calendar, the SLA, and per-cycle reports for the file. An organised vendor produces these as routine; a disorganised one treats each as a special request. The state of a vendor’s paperwork predicts the state of their scheduling with surprising accuracy.

9. “How long have you existed, and where?”

AC maintenance is a business with almost no barriers to entry and brutal summer attrition — outfits appear in March and evaporate by October, taking your annual fee’s remaining visits with them. Years in operation, a fixed address you can visit, and referenceable clients in your sector are the boring credentials that matter. (Ours: since 1987, East of Kailash, the history is here.)

10. “What will you find wrong in the first month?”

A revealing question. A serious vendor answers with a process: a baseline assessment of every unit, measured, before the contract starts — because they know unmaintained estates hide problems and they want them on paper on day one, priced and prioritised, rather than discovered mid-contract as disputes. A vendor who answers “nothing, we’ll keep it all running” is selling you reassurance. (This is exactly what the facility assessment exists for.)

Normalise the quotes before comparing them

Once the interviews are done, resist comparing bottom-line numbers directly — the quotes almost certainly describe different products. Build a one-page grid: per-unit basis, tier, visits per year with months, itemised visit scope, response time in writing, reporting commitment, exclusions, renewal terms. Fill it from the documents, not the meetings — anything promised verbally but absent from paper goes in as “no.” Most fields will turn at least one attractive price into an obviously thinner product, and occasionally the expensive quote reveals itself as the only one actually offering maintenance. The grid also becomes your procurement file’s justification page when someone later asks why the cheapest bid lost.

Red flags that end the evaluation early

Some answers save you the remaining questions:

  • A flat per-unit price quoted before anyone has seen your site. Either the price carries a fat ignorance margin or the exclusions clause will do the real work later.
  • “Gas top-up included every year.” Refrigerant does not get consumed; a contract that budgets for routine top-ups is telling you it does not intend to repair leaks — it intends to farm them.
  • No fixed office address, or one that turns out to be a flat. The vendors who vanish in October all had impressive phone manners in March.
  • Reluctance to put the response time in the contract. Verbal SLAs are weather forecasts.
  • A price dramatically below the field. In a labour business, a 40%-cheaper quote is not efficiency — it is fewer visits, thinner scope or staff who will not stay the summer. You are not finding value; you are pre-paying for a dispute.

Structure the first 90 days as a test

However good the answers, contract like an engineer: verify. Insist the engagement opens with a baseline assessment (every unit measured and reported before the schedule starts), put the first two scheduled visits in the calendar with dates at signing, and diarise a 90-day review against three checks — did the visits happen on the dates, did the per-unit reports arrive unprompted, and did any breakdown in the period meet the SLA. A vendor who performs under inspection in the first quarter usually keeps performing; one who slips in the honeymoon quarter has shown you the whole movie.

Scoring it

Weight questions 1, 2 and 3 above everything — reporting, measured scope, and an enforceable SLA are the contract; the rest is logistics. If a vendor clears those three and their price is 15% higher than one who does not, the higher price is the cheaper contract. The one thing you should never do is average the quotes: in this market the median bid is a wash-and-wipe service with a friendly invoice, and June does not grade on a curve.
