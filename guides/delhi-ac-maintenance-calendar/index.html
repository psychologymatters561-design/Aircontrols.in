AC maintenance schedule for Delhi’s climate, month by month

Dust storms in May, a steam bath till September, smog all winter — Delhi attacks air conditioning in sequence. The calendar that answers it, month by month, with the two service windows that decide your summer.

Delhi does not have a climate so much as a sequence of climates: a dusty spring, a furnace, a steam bath, a pleasant autumn and a cold, smoggy winter — each of which attacks air conditioning differently. A maintenance schedule copied from a manufacturer’s global manual misses all of it. Here is the calendar as it actually applies here, month by month, for homes and facilities alike.

January – February: decide, quote, contract

The machines are resting; this is the buyer’s window. Fix contracts, compare vendors (how to evaluate one), schedule the pre-summer service for units under contract, and plan replacements flagged last season — installers are unhurried and available now, and will not be in May. Winter smog has one direct effect worth knowing: Delhi’s high winter particulate load settles into idle outdoor units, joining the year’s dust on the fins.

February – March: the pre-summer service — the one that matters most

If your year contains one professional service, it happens now, and it decides your June. The full scope: coils washed, filters serviced, drains flushed and verified, electrical connections tightened, capacitor measured against its rating, gas pressure measured against specification, fan and thermostat verified — the whole nine-point checklist, with readings recorded. The point of the timing: every weakness this service finds — a drifting capacitor, a slow leak, a fouled coil — gets fixed at leisure prices in March instead of failing at emergency prices in June.

Most summer breakdowns are decided in winter.

April: the shakedown

Heat arrives; units go from idle to daily duty. The first week of real load is when marginal units declare themselves — weak cooling, long cycles, ice, trips. Treat any symptom in April as a gift: you have caught in week one what would otherwise fail in week ten. Homes: start the monthly filter rinse now and keep it up through June. Facilities: watch the complaint log’s first entries — they map your weakest units.

May – June: the furnace, and the dust storms

45°C afternoons, units running 10–14 hours, condensers rejecting heat into air that is already hotter than body temperature — this is the design limit, run daily. Two Delhi specifics: pre-monsoon dust storms can deposit a season’s fouling on condenser coils in one evening — after a big one, rinse accessible outdoor coils (power off) or have them washed; and the grid sags and spikes under city-wide load, which is what kills marginal capacitors and PCBs. Keep filters clean monthly, keep condenser airflow clear, and do not park anything hot (generators, kitchen exhaust) near an outdoor unit. If a unit is limping now, diagnose it properly — in these weeks a blind gas top-up is the most commonly mis-sold repair in the city.

July – September: the steam bath

Monsoon halves the temperature problem and doubles the water problem. Humidity means every unit runs heavy latent loads — dehumidifying as much as cooling — and condensate volumes peak. The season’s characteristic failures are all water: blocked drains overflowing indoors, algae and biofilm growing in warm drain pans, musty odours from biofilm on wet evaporators, and corrosion beginning wherever the pre-monsoon dust paste sits on wet metal. The dust the storms deposited in June now bonds to fins as a crust that plain water will not shift. Mid-contract visits in these months focus on drains, pans and coil hygiene — and clinics should note the air-quality dimension of wet, dirty coils.

October: the post-monsoon service

The second scheduled service of the contract year, and nearly as consequential as February’s. The machines have just run six hard months; the monsoon has left bonded grime, biofilm and incipient corrosion. Scope now: chemical or pressure coil cleaning to strip the monsoon crust, drain and pan disinfection, corrosion inspection and treatment, electrical checks after a season of voltage abuse, and a full set of readings — which, compared against March’s, tells you exactly what the summer cost each unit. Units heading into winter shutdown get their closing condition recorded, so spring surprises have a paper trail.

November – December: shutdown and the long idle

Comfort cooling goes quiet (server rooms, process cooling and cold storage obviously exempt — their cycles continue year-round). For idle units: clean filters before shutdown rather than after — stored grime plus winter damp is a biology experiment; cover outdoor units only with breathable covers, since sealed plastic traps condensation against the metal all winter; and run each idle unit briefly on a mild December afternoon — ten minutes of circulation keeps seals wetted and bearings moving, and surfaces any fault while repair diaries are empty.

If you only do three things

For a home, the whole calendar compresses to this: a professional full service in February or March before the load arrives; filters rinsed monthly, April through June, which costs nothing and protects everything downstream; and a post-monsoon service in October to strip what the wet season bonded onto the coils. A home that does only these three enters each summer with the odds rearranged in its favour — most of the June breakdowns we attend belong to machines that got none of them. The rest of the calendar is refinement; these three are the structure, and a maintenance plan exists mainly so they happen on schedule without anyone’s diary being involved.

The same calendar, read from a facility manager’s chair

For an office, clinic or multi-unit building, the months carry an extra planning layer:

  • January is budget month. The October readings — what summer cost each unit, which are on the watch-list — are your evidence for the maintenance and replacement line items. Estates that budget from data argue less in June.
  • February’s service is a scheduling project. Twenty-plus units means zones, off-hours windows and sign-offs; book the contractor’s calendar in January, not when the first hot week arrives and everyone else does.
  • April’s complaint log is an instrument. The first fortnight of real load maps your marginal units better than any survey — feed it back into the visit plan rather than treating each complaint as a one-off.
  • October is contract-review month. The season just ended is the only honest test a vendor gets: visits on the promised dates, reports delivered unprompted, SLA met on breakdowns. Renew — or run the vendor evaluation — while the evidence is fresh, months before the next contract cycle.
