Why your AC stopped cooling: 9 causes, in order of likelihood

From a choked filter to a dead compressor — the nine causes a Delhi service engineer checks, in the order they actually occur, with what you can check yourself before paying anyone.

An AC that runs but does not cool has a short list of possible causes, and they are not equally likely. Below are the nine, in the order a Delhi service engineer actually finds them — with what you can check yourself before calling anyone, and what the fix involves when you do. Work the list in order; the cheap causes are also the common ones.

1. Choked air filter

The most common and the only fully DIY item. Delhi’s dust loads a filter in weeks during peak season. A choked filter starves the evaporator of airflow: cooling drops, the coil can ice over, and power draw rises. Open the front panel, rinse the filters, dry, refit — monthly from April to June. If the filter was visibly caked and cooling returns, you found it.

2. Fouled condenser coil

The outdoor unit’s job is to reject heat; a coil blinded by dust, lint and street film cannot. The compressor works harder and hotter, draws 15–30% more power, cooling falls, and on the worst afternoons the unit trips on thermal protection. You can rinse loose dust off gently with water (power off), but a properly fouled coil needs pressure-washing or chemical cleaning — part of every deep service. If your outdoor unit sits in a service lane or near a road, assume this cause by default. What the fouling costs you in electricity is its own guide.

3. Low refrigerant — which means a leak

Refrigerant is not petrol; the system does not consume it. If the charge is low, it left through a leak — usually a flare joint, a corroded bend or a rubbed-through capillary. Symptoms: weak cooling that worsens over weeks, ice on the thin copper pipe, occasionally a hissing indoor unit. The correct repair sequence is non-negotiable: find the leak, repair it, pressure-test, then recharge to the manufacturer’s specification. A top-up without the repair is the same bill again next month, plus the slow damage of running a leaking system. Anyone offering to “just fill gas” without leak detection is not doing you the favour it sounds like.

4. Dirty evaporator coil

The indoor coil clogs more slowly than the filter in front of it, but after a few unserviced seasons it fouls with fine dust and biofilm, and heat transfer collapses. The unit blows air that is only mildly cool, and the room never reaches set point. This one is not DIY: cleaning an evaporator properly means opening the unit, protecting the electricals and washing the coil — standard scope in any real service.

5. Weak or failed capacitor

The run capacitor gives the compressor and fan motors their starting torque. Capacitors age chemically — Delhi’s heat accelerates it — and a capacitor that has drifted well below its rated microfarads leaves the compressor humming but not starting, or starting and quickly tripping. Classic signs: the indoor unit blows room-temperature air while the outdoor fan spins but the compressor does not kick, or the outdoor unit clicks and goes quiet. The fix is cheap (₹500–₹1,500 fitted) if caught before the repeated failed starts damage the compressor — which is exactly why a measured capacitor reading is in every proper service checklist.

6. Thermostat or sensor fault

A sensor reading wrong tells the compressor to stop early or never engage. Symptoms are behavioural rather than mechanical: cooling that cuts out long before the room is cool, wild short-cycling, or a unit that ignores its set point. Sometimes it is genuinely the remote’s settings (dry mode, fan-only, a 28°C set point someone else chose) — check that first. A real sensor fault is a small part, diagnosed with a meter.

7. Frozen evaporator coil

Ice on the indoor coil is a symptom with two usual parents: airflow starvation (causes 1 and 4) or low refrigerant (cause 3). The ice itself then blocks what airflow remained, and the unit blows feeble, vaguely cool air. Switch the unit off and let it thaw — then treat the cause, because ice always has one. Running a freezing unit floods the compressor with liquid refrigerant risk.

8. The heat load exceeds the machine

Some “failures” are arithmetic. A west-facing room under an uninsulated Delhi roof in a 45°C week can defeat a unit that was adequate in March — especially if tonnage was chosen optimistically, doors stay open, or occupancy doubled. Signs: the unit runs continuously, everything measures healthy, and the room stabilises a few degrees above set point during peak afternoons only. The fixes are load-side: shading, sealing, ceiling insulation, or accepting the unit needs help. No repair will change physics.

9. Compressor failure

Last on the list because it is the least common — and usually the terminal event of causes that went unfixed: years of fouled-coil overheating, repeated hard starts on a weak capacitor, running low on gas. Diagnosis is definitive (winding resistance, current draw, pressures), and the decision is economic, not technical: on an ageing unit, a compressor replacement at ₹8,000–₹18,000 competes with the price of a new, far more efficient machine. Repair or replace walks through that arithmetic honestly.

What to tell the engineer on the phone

Whoever you call, three observations from you cut the diagnosis time in half:

  • What the outdoor unit is doing. Fan spinning but no compressor hum points at the capacitor or electricals; everything running but weak cooling points at coils or gas; totally dead points at supply or PCB.
  • The timeline. “Gradually worse over three weeks” is a coil or a slow leak; “fine yesterday, dead today” is electrical; “only fails on the hottest afternoons” is a marginal capacitor, a fouled condenser or a load problem.
  • Any ice, water or smells. Ice on the thin pipe, water indoors, a musty or chemical smell — each removes half the list before anyone opens a panel.

And one caution for June: describe the symptoms, but be wary of any diagnosis delivered confidently over the phone, sight unseen, that happens to be the most expensive item on this list. “Compressor gone, sir” is a sentence that should always be accompanied by a meter in someone’s hand.

The pattern behind the list

Read the nine again and notice what they have in common: almost none of them begins on the day the cooling stops. Filters and coils foul over months; leaks seep over seasons; capacitors drift for a year before the morning they fail. The breakdown is the invoice for maintenance that did not happen — which is the entire argument for scheduled maintenance with measurements, made better than any brochure could. In Delhi specifically, the two service windows that break this cycle are February–March and October — the reasoning is in the month-by-month calendar.
