How much electricity a dirty AC coil actually wastes

15–30% extra power draw, measurable on a clamp meter. The mechanism in one minute, then the season’s arithmetic in rupees — for one split, and for a 25-unit office estate.

Of all the arguments for AC maintenance, this one has the cleanest numbers: a dirty coil makes the compressor draw 15–30% more power for the same cooling. Not vaguely “less efficient” — measurably more current, on a clamp meter, on your units, every running hour. This guide explains the mechanism and then does the arithmetic in rupees, because the coil does not care whether you believe in maintenance.

The mechanism, in one minute

An air conditioner is a heat pump: it absorbs heat from the room at the indoor (evaporator) coil and rejects it outdoors at the condenser coil. Both jobs depend on air flowing freely over thin metal fins. Dust, lint, street film and (on kitchen-adjacent units) grease coat those fins and behave as insulation exactly where the machine needs conduction.

When the condenser fouls, the system cannot reject heat, so refrigerant pressure and temperature on the high side climb. The compressor — the component that consumes almost all of an AC’s power — must work against that higher head pressure: more current for less delivered cooling, running hotter, for longer, closer to its thermal limits. When the evaporator fouls, the cold side starves: heat absorption falls, the coil over-cools itself (ice risk), and the unit runs longer cycles to move less heat.

Both degradations are gradual, which is why nobody notices. The unit still cools — it just takes an extra 40 minutes to do it, at higher draw, twelve hours a day, for a season.

The arithmetic for one Delhi split

Take an ordinary 1.5-ton split drawing about 1.5 kW while running, cooling a Delhi room through the long season — call it 8 running hours a day for 180 days, April into September. That is roughly 2,160 kWh a season when healthy.

Now the comparison that matters: a deep clean with coil wash costs ₹800–₹1,500. The typical-fouling row alone repays that two or three times over in one season — before counting the breakdown risk, and this is the arithmetic for one unit.

Now multiply by an office

A 25-unit office estate with typical fouling is wasting on the order of 10,000 kWh a season — roughly ₹75,000–₹80,000 at commercial tariffs, silently, spread across an electricity bill where nobody can see it. That figure is frequently larger than the entire annual cost of the maintenance contract that would eliminate it. Facility managers looking for a budget argument for the AMC line item: this is it, and it is auditable — current draw before and after cleaning is a two-minute clamp-meter reading, which is why our condition reports record it.

Why Delhi fouls coils faster than almost anywhere

  • Dust load. Delhi’s PM and road dust levels are among the highest of any major city, and pre-monsoon dust storms deposit a season’s grime in an afternoon.
  • Where condensers live. Service lanes, light wells and packed rooftops — recirculating their own hot exhaust through a rising film of street dust.
  • Monsoon after dust. Humidity turns the deposited dust into a bonded paste on the fins, then supports biofilm on the wet evaporator. The post-monsoon service exists because of this sequence — see the Delhi maintenance calendar.
  • Kitchen and industrial air. Grease and process exhaust bond aggressively to fins and will not rinse off with water — restaurant and industrial-area units need shorter, chemical cleaning cycles.

How to spot a fouled coil without instruments

You do not need gauges to get a strong hint; the machine advertises its condition:

  • Look at the condenser fins (outdoor unit, power off). Healthy fins show metal; fouled ones show a grey-brown felt. If you cannot see light through the coil from the back, the compressor cannot push heat through it either.
  • Feel the condenser’s discharge air. A healthy condenser blows distinctly hot air — that is your room’s heat leaving. Weak, mildly warm discharge with the compressor running means the heat is not transferring; it is staying in the system.
  • Time a cooldown. If the same room that reached set point in 25 minutes in April needs an hour in June-like conditions it handled fine last year, the machine is telling you its heat exchange has degraded.
  • Watch the electricity bill’s shape. Consumption creeping up year-on-year for the same weather and usage is the fouling showing up in accounts before it shows up as a breakdown.

One nuance for newer machines: an inverter AC hides fouling better than a fixed-speed unit — instead of failing to reach set point, it quietly runs at higher compressor speeds and higher draw to compensate. The comfort stays; only the meter and the compressor’s lifespan pay. Which is an argument for measuring rather than waiting for symptoms, since the symptom an inverter shows first is the bill.

The compounding cost nobody bills you for

The electricity is the visible waste. The invisible one: a compressor working against high head pressure runs hot, and heat is what ages compressors, windings and capacitors. The fouled coil you carry for two seasons does not just cost ₹6,000 in power — it pulls the compressor’s failure forward into its most expensive possible week. When that failure arrives, the decision it forces is covered in repair or replace.

Water, pressure or chemicals: matching the clean to the fouling

“Coil cleaning” covers three different jobs, and using the wrong one either wastes money or damages fins:

  • Plain water rinse — adequate for loose, recent dust on a condenser, and the only method a non-professional should attempt (power off, gentle flow, never a high-pressure jet held close — bent fins are their own airflow blockage).
  • Pressure washing — the standard professional clean for a season’s accumulation, done at controlled pressure and angle so fins survive it, with the unit’s electricals masked.
  • Chemical / foam cleaning — for bonded fouling: the post-monsoon crust, kitchen grease, industrial film, and evaporator biofilm. An alkaline foam lifts what water cannot, then rinses out. This is what “chemical wash” means on a service menu, and it is the right spend for lane-facing condensers and restaurant units roughly once a year.

Frequency, honestly stated: filters monthly in season (yours to do), a professional deep clean pre-summer, a second post-monsoon — and shorter cycles only where the environment demands it, as measured by the coil’s graded condition, not by a salesman’s calendar.

What actually keeps coils clean

Filters you can and should rinse monthly in season yourself. Coils are the professional item: evaporators need the unit opened and washed with the electricals protected; condensers need pressure or chemical cleaning matched to the fouling. The realistic cadence in Delhi is a pre-summer deep clean plus a post-monsoon service, with lane-facing and kitchen-adjacent units on shorter cycles — which is, not coincidentally, exactly the calendar a maintenance contract runs, with the coil’s condition graded in writing each visit so the cleaning happens when the fins need it, not when the calendar guesses.
