Comprehensive vs non-comprehensive AC AMC: what’s covered, what isn’t

The two names hide one question: who pays when a part fails. Definitions, a side-by-side coverage table, which tier fits which estate, and the five clauses to read before you compare any prices.

Every AC AMC quote in Delhi comes in two (sometimes three) flavours, and the names are used loosely enough that buyers regularly sign the wrong one. The distinction is simple once stated plainly: who pays for parts and gas when something fails — you, or the contractor.

The three cover levels, defined

Non-comprehensive AMC

The contractor commits to the service programme: scheduled visits on a fixed calendar, the full servicing scope each visit, and labour for breakdown calls. When a part fails or gas is needed, the material is billed to you at agreed rates, usually with a parts discount. You carry the repair risk; in exchange, the annual fee is the lowest — this is the baseline price in the pricing guide.

Semi-comprehensive AMC

The middle path. Everything in non-comprehensive, plus minor parts — capacitors, relays, filters — and typically a limited gas allowance (for example, one top-up per unit per year after leak repair). Major components — compressor, PCB, fan motor — remain billable. Priced roughly 1.5 to 1.7 times the non-comprehensive rate.

Comprehensive AMC

The contractor covers parts and refrigerant within written limits, so your annual figure is close to fully fixed. Priced around 2 to 2.5 times non-comprehensive. Read the exclusions clause before comparing prices: virtually every honest comprehensive contract in the market still excludes compressor replacement and damage from external causes (voltage surges beyond protection, physical damage, water ingress). A “comprehensive” contract that claims to exclude nothing is priced for a dispute.

The clause that decides more disputes than any other: gas

Whatever tier you sign, read the refrigerant clause twice, because it is where the market’s worst behaviour concentrates. The physics is simple — refrigerant circulates in a sealed loop and does not get used up; if it is low, it escaped through a leak — so a well-written clause follows the physics: leak found and repaired, pressure-tested, then recharged, with any quantity cap stated per unit per year. Two wordings should stop you signing. “Annual gas top-up included” sounds generous and means the opposite: it budgets for the leak to stay. And “gas charged extra at actuals” with no leak-repair obligation lets a contractor bill you for the same escaping gas twice a season indefinitely — the cheapest AMC in the stack often finances itself exactly this way.

Can you change tiers later?

Yes, and the natural direction is upward with age: estates commonly start non-comprehensive while young and move to semi or comprehensive as they cross the 5–7 year line and parts failures become statistics rather than surprises. The switch normally happens at renewal, and an honest contractor will re-inspect before writing the higher tier — they are taking on your parts risk and are entitled to see what they are underwriting. Mid-term upgrades are possible but rarer; mid-term downgrades are a warning sign in the other direction: a contractor pushing you down-tier after a bad claims year is repricing their mistake onto your next breakdown.

Which one should you take?

Take non-comprehensive when…

  • Your units are young — under 5–7 years. Failure rates are low, so paying a premium to insure against failures you are unlikely to have is poor arithmetic.
  • You have the cash-flow tolerance for an occasional parts bill and want the lowest fixed cost.
  • You mainly want the discipline: the calendar, the protocol, the reports — which is where most of an AMC’s value actually lives.

Take comprehensive when…

  • The estate is ageing — 7+ years — and parts failures are a matter of when, not if. You are converting an unpredictable repair stream into a fixed line item.
  • Budget certainty matters institutionally: a clinic, a school, a company where an unbudgeted ₹40,000 across a summer is an escalation, not an inconvenience.
  • Downtime intolerance is high. When the contractor owns the parts cost, the contractor has every incentive to prevent the failure — the incentives align with yours.

Semi-comprehensive earns its keep when…

The estate is mixed-age, and the failure statistics are dominated by exactly what it covers: capacitors and small electricals are the most common AC failures in Delhi’s voltage-and-heat environment, and slow leaks needing an occasional top-up are the second. For many offices and homes, covering those two categories removes 80% of the surprise bills for half the comprehensive premium.

The clauses to read before price

  1. The gas clause. “Gas included” should mean: leak found, repaired, pressure-tested, then recharged — with any quantity cap stated. Gas topped up without a leak repair is not a benefit; it is the same bill deferred a month.
  2. The exclusions list. Should be short, specific and written. Vague exclusions (“major repairs extra”) turn every large failure into a negotiation.
  3. The visit calendar. How many scheduled services, in which months, covering what scope — see what’s included in an AC AMC for the checklist a visit should run.
  4. The reporting. Per-unit written condition reports are what let you verify the contract is being executed at all. No reporting, no contract — at any tier.
  5. Old-unit terms. Honest contractors inspect before writing comprehensive cover on 10–12+ year units, or decline it. Be suspicious of anyone eager to comprehensively insure a machine they have never opened.

Two worked examples

Case A: a 6-unit office, all splits, 2–4 years old

Failure probability on a young estate is low: statistically perhaps one small electrical item and no major failures in a year. Non-comprehensive at, say, ₹2,400 average per unit (₹14,400/year) with a ₹1,000–₹1,500 occasional parts bill beats comprehensive at roughly ₹36,000 by a wide margin — you would be paying ~₹20,000 a year to insure against failures the estate is unlikely to produce. Take non-comprehensive; bank the difference; revisit at year six.

Case B: a 15-unit clinic estate, mostly 8–11 years old

Now the failure statistics invert. On an estate this age, several capacitor-class failures, a fan motor or PCB event, and one or two leak repairs in a year are the expected case, not the unlucky one — call it ₹20,000–₹40,000 of unbudgeted repairs, arriving preferentially in peak weeks, in a facility where downtime has clinical consequences. The comprehensive premium over non-comprehensive (roughly ₹1.3–1.5× the non-comp fee in absolute terms) buys a fixed number, aligned incentives and first-priority response. Take comprehensive — after the contractor inspects, which at this age they should insist on.

The same decision, seen from the contractor’s side

It clarifies the choice to know how the other side prices it. On non-comprehensive, the contractor sells a service programme — margin is in efficiency of scheduling. On comprehensive, the contractor is underwriting your estate: the premium over non-comprehensive is an insurance calculation on your units’ age, condition and duty. That is why a serious contractor insists on a baseline assessment before quoting comprehensive cover — and why a quote produced without one should worry you: either the price has a large ignorance margin built in, or the exclusions clause will be doing the real work when a claim arrives.
