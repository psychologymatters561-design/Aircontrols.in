What’s included in an AC annual maintenance contract (with the checklist)

Two AMCs with the same price can describe completely different amounts of maintenance. The seven components of a real contract, the nine-point visit checklist with the measurements that matter, and the exclusions that should not surprise you.

“AMC” is doing a lot of unsupervised work in the Delhi market. Two contracts with the same name and similar prices can describe completely different amounts of actual maintenance — one a measured engineering programme, the other twelve months of access to a phone number. This guide lists what a real AC Annual Maintenance Contract includes, item by item, so you can hold any quote — including ours — against it.

1. A written unit inventory

The contract should name what it covers: every unit, with type, tonnage, location, brand and age. This sounds bureaucratic and is actually the foundation — without an inventory, “all ACs serviced” is unverifiable, and the units behind the storeroom door quietly fall out of scope. On multi-unit sites the inventory is also what makes per-unit history possible: unit AC-014 has a file, not a vague memory.

2. A fixed visit calendar

Minimum two scheduled services per unit per year for comfort cooling in Delhi: a pre-summer service in February–March — the one that decides your June — and a post-monsoon service to deal with what humidity, dust and four months of continuous running have done. High-duty units — server rooms, clinical spaces, cold storage, kitchen-adjacent plant — belong on quarterly or monthly cycles. The calendar goes in the contract with months named. “We’ll come twice” without dates means the pre-summer visit happens in May, in the queue, defeating its purpose. The reasoning month by month is in the Delhi AC maintenance calendar.

3. The service checklist itself

This is the heart of the contract, and where quotes differ most while sounding identical. “Full service” should be an itemised list. Ours is nine points, and the right-hand column is the part that matters — the measurement, not the action:

#ItemWhat is actually done and recorded
01Evaporator coilCleaned; condition rated and recorded
02Condenser coilCleaned, fins straightened; condition rated
03Air filterServiced or replaced; airflow restored
04Condensate drainFlushed and cleared; flow verified
05Electrical connectionsChecked and tightened; terminals inspected
06CapacitorMicrofarad value measured and recorded against rating
07Refrigerant pressureMeasured against manufacturer specification, recorded
08Fan motor & bladeWear, bearing noise and balance inspected
09ThermostatCalibration tested against measured air temperature

Two items deserve emphasis because they are the ones cheap services skip. The capacitor reading: a capacitor drifting below its rated microfarads is the single most common cause of peak-summer compressor start failure, and it costs nothing to measure during a service — but only if someone actually puts a meter on it. And refrigerant pressure against specification: “gas checked” by touching the pipe is folklore; a gauge reading against the manufacturer’s spec, written down, catches slow leaks months before the unit stops cooling.

4. Written condition reports

After each scheduled visit: a per-unit report with the readings, condition grades for coils and components, and a prioritised action list — what needs doing now, what should be planned, what to budget for. This document is how you verify the contract is being executed, how disputes are avoided, and how repair-or-replace decisions get made on trend data instead of opinion. The format we use is published here. A contract without written reporting is not a maintenance contract; it is a subscription to reassurance.

5. Breakdown response with a number on it

The AMC should state a response time — ours is 2 hours for commercial sites, 4 for homes — and an escalation path if it slips. “Priority response” without a number is a mood, not a commitment. In practice this clause is worth the most in exactly the weeks it is hardest to honour: May and June, when non-contract customers wait days.

6. Clear terms for parts and gas

Whichever tier you choose — and the tiers are compared properly in comprehensive vs non-comprehensive — the contract should state how materials are handled: what is included, what is billed, at what discount, and the non-negotiable discipline that gas is only recharged after the leak is found and repaired. A contract that budgets for routine annual top-ups is quietly telling you it does not intend to fix leaks.

7. The exclusions, in writing

Every honest AMC excludes something — typically compressor replacement and damage from external causes. The test of a good contract is not the absence of exclusions but their specificity. Short, concrete exclusions protect both sides; vague ones (“major repairs charged extra”) turn every significant failure into a negotiation you will lose in June.

How many services per year is “correct”?

The question gets asked as if there were one number; there are three, by duty:

Duty classScheduled servicesExamples
Comfort cooling, seasonal2 / yearHomes, cabins, meeting rooms, most office floors
Heavy or extended duty3–4 / yearRestaurants and kitchen-adjacent units, showrooms, coworking floors, dusty or lane-facing condensers
Continuous / critical duty4–12 / yearServer and UPS rooms, clinical spaces, cold storage, process cooling

Beware of both ends of the market on this: the cut-price AMC that quietly means one visit a year (the pre-summer visit alone leaves the monsoon’s water problems unattended), and the padded contract selling monthly visits to a three-split office — frequency beyond what the duty demands is billing, not maintenance.

And when something breaks between visits?

A breakdown visit under a real AMC looks like a compressed service visit, not a patch job: the fault is diagnosed with instruments, the repair is made under the contract’s terms for labour and parts, and — the part cheap operators skip — the unit’s readings are taken after the repair and added to its history, because a failure is data. If the same unit fails twice, the history is what turns the second call-out into a root-cause conversation instead of a coincidence.

What is usually not included — and shouldn’t surprise you

  • Compressor replacement — the one big-ticket item almost universally excluded; see repair or replace for how to decide when it fails.
  • Civil and structural work — relocating units, building modifications, electrical wiring beyond the AC’s own connections.
  • Consumables damaged by external causes — surges beyond protection, physical damage, rodent damage to insulation and cabling (a genuinely common Delhi failure).
  • Duct cleaning and air-quality work on ducted systems — related, but a separately scoped job.

Duration, start dates and renewal

Contracts run twelve months, and when they start matters more than buyers realise: a contract signed in January gets its pre-summer service in February–March as designed, while one signed in June starts with a season of firefighting on units nobody baselined. If you are signing mid-year, expect (and welcome) an opening assessment and possibly remedial work billed once, separately — that is the contractor refusing to pretend your estate is in a condition it is not. At renewal, the year’s condition reports are your negotiation file: they show what was done, what was found and what the estate now needs, which is a better basis for the year-two price than either side’s memory.

The one-paragraph test

Hold any AMC quote against this: Does it name the units, fix the calendar, itemise the visit scope with measurements, promise written per-unit reports, put a number on response time, state material terms, and list its exclusions specifically? If yes, you are comparing real contracts and can argue about price. If no, the missing item is the price — you just pay it later, in June, at emergency rates.
