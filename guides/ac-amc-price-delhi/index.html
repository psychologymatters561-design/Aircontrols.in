AC AMC price in Delhi: what a maintenance contract actually costs

From ₹1,800 per AC per year to five-figure comprehensive contracts — the five variables that honestly set the number, what a fair quote must state, and the arithmetic against paying per breakdown.

“What does an AC AMC cost in Delhi?” has a short answer and an honest answer. The short answer: from about ₹1,800 per AC per year for a basic (non-comprehensive) plan on a newer 1-ton split, rising to ₹8,000–₹9,000+ per unit for comprehensive cover on larger or ageing machines — with commercial, multi-unit contracts priced per site. The honest answer is that the number is built from five variables, and any contractor quoting you a single flat rate without asking about them is either guessing or planning to recover the difference later.

The five variables that set the price

1. Unit type and tonnage

Maintenance effort scales with the machine. A 1-ton split is an hour’s careful work; a 2-ton unit has more coil area, more refrigerant and a heavier electrical section; cassettes are slower to open and close properly; ducted and VRV/VRF plant is a different discipline entirely. As working reference points for Delhi residential plans, per unit per year on a basic (non-comprehensive) plan:

UnitTypical basic-plan price
1-ton split (newer)₹1,800–₹2,200
1.5-ton split₹2,200–₹2,800
2-ton split₹2,800–₹3,800
3-ton / light commercial₹3,800–₹5,500

Cassette, ducted and VRV/VRF equipment is always priced after inspection — the installed configuration matters more than the nameplate.

2. Age of the unit

Age is the honest multiplier most quotes hide. A unit in years 0–3 rarely needs more than clean-and-verify. By years 7–12, capacitors drift, coils have accumulated years of Delhi’s air, valves and joints have had thousands of thermal cycles — the same contract carries perhaps 1.5 times the work. Past year 12, some contractors will not offer comprehensive cover at all without an inspection first, and that is the correct behaviour: a contract priced without looking at an old unit is a contract designed to be argued about later.

3. Cover level

The largest single swing. A non-comprehensive AMC covers scheduled servicing and labour; parts and gas are billed as used. A comprehensive AMC folds parts and gas (within written limits) into the fixed fee — typically 2 to 2.5 times the non-comprehensive rate for the same unit. Neither is “better”; they place the repair risk on different sides of the table. The full breakdown is in comprehensive vs non-comprehensive AC AMC.

4. Number of units

Per-unit prices fall as counts rise, because travel, scheduling and admin spread across the estate. Volume discounts of 8% (2–5 units) up to the high twenties (20+ units) are normal and you should expect them in writing. This is also why a 30-unit office should never pay thirty times a single-home rate — and why serious multi-unit work is quoted as a commercial contract after a site walk.

5. Duty cycle

An AC cooling a bedroom six hours a night and one holding a server room at 22°C around the clock are not the same maintenance proposition, whatever the nameplate says. Round-the-clock and process-critical units need quarterly or monthly attention, and the contract price carries that visit count.

What a fair quote looks like on paper

Whatever the number, a quote worth signing states: the unit inventory it covers (type, tonnage, age per unit), the visit calendar, what each visit includes, the response time for breakdowns, what is excluded, and how parts and gas are priced when they are needed. A one-line quote — “AMC for 10 ACs: ₹X” — is not a price, it is an opening position.

The arithmetic against “we just call when it breaks”

Run the comparison the way a facilities buyer would. A peak-season emergency on one 1.5-ton split — say a failed capacitor that took the compressor’s start circuit with it, plus a gas top-up after the resulting strain found the weak joint — lands at ₹4,000–₹7,000 in parts and urgency, plus days of a hot room or office in the queue behind every other June breakdown in Delhi. One such event costs more than a year of basic cover on that unit; two events cost more than a comprehensive plan. And the failure pattern is not random: unmaintained units fail precisely in the weeks when they are working hardest and every contractor is busiest.

An AMC pays for itself on the first breakdown it prevents.

There is a second, quieter line in the arithmetic: energy. A fouled coil forces the compressor to draw 15–30% more power for the same cooling — on an estate of units, that is a standing surcharge that scheduled cleaning removes. The kWh arithmetic is here.

How commercial pricing differs from the home rates above

The residential tables scale only so far. A commercial quote is built differently, and it is worth knowing how, because it explains why serious contractors will not quote a 30-unit office over the phone:

  • It starts from an inventory, not a headcount. “Thirty ACs” might be thirty newish 1.5-ton splits, or twelve splits, ten cassettes, six ageing ducted zones and two server-room units on 24/7 duty. Those are different contracts with legitimately different prices — which is why the first step is a walk-through that records every unit.
  • The visit calendar is priced, not assumed. A comfort-cooling estate on two visits a year and an estate with quarterly server-room checks and monthly kitchen-unit cycles carry different labour loads, and the quote should show it.
  • The SLA has a price. A written 2-hour response with an escalation path means the contractor holds capacity in reserve through peak season. Quotes that match on per-unit rate but differ on response commitment are not the same product.
  • Baseline condition moves the first year. An estate that has not been properly serviced in years needs remedial work — deep cleans, overdue repairs — before it can be maintained economically. An honest quote separates that one-time catch-up from the recurring annual rate rather than burying it or, worse, discovering it in month three.

The practical sequence for a commercial buyer: a paid facility assessment first, which produces the inventory and the per-unit condition baseline, then a contract quote built on it — with the assessment fee credited against the contract. Anything else is pricing by guesswork, in one direction or the other.

When an AMC is not worth it

Honesty cuts both ways. A single lightly-used window unit in a rented room does not need a contract — an annual paid service covers it. A unit past its economic life (see repair or replace) should be replaced, not contracted. And if a vendor’s AMC does not include written per-unit reporting, you are buying a discount card, not maintenance — walk away regardless of price.

Five questions that test any price

  1. “Per unit, or per site?” A lump sum with no per-unit basis cannot be checked when units are added, removed or replaced mid-contract — and estates always change.
  2. “Which tier is this, exactly?” If the quote does not say non-comprehensive, semi or comprehensive in writing, the coverage is whatever the contractor decides it is when something fails.
  3. “What would this same estate cost on the other tiers?” A contractor who can price all three quickly understands their own cost model. One who can only produce the number they led with is reading from a rate card that ignores your estate.
  4. “What is the price at renewal?” Year-one teaser rates with unstated renewal escalation are common. Ask for the renewal basis in writing.
  5. “What did you assume about my units’ condition?” The answer tells you whether the price has an ignorance margin hidden in it — and whether the first dispute is already scheduled.
