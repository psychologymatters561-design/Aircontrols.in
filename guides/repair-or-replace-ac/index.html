When to repair and when to replace an old air conditioner

The decision is economic and takes five minutes with honest inputs: the age-weighted repair rule, the efficiency term most advice ignores, the R22 phase-out, and the cases where repair wins anyway.

Every failed AC eventually puts the same question on the table: put money into this machine, or into its successor? The answer is economic, not sentimental, and it can be worked out in five minutes with honest inputs. Here is the framework we use when clients ask us — including the cases where the answer is “repair it, whatever the internet says.”

Start with the three numbers

  1. The repair quote — diagnosed, not guessed. A capacitor is ₹500–₹1,500; a PCB ₹2,500–₹8,000; a compressor ₹8,000–₹18,000 installed (published ranges).
  2. The replacement cost — a new 1.5-ton 5-star split lands in the ₹35,000–₹55,000 range installed, brand depending.
  3. The unit’s age — against a practical Delhi lifespan of 10–15 years for a decently maintained split, less for units that have run hard and unserviced.

The 50% rule, with a Delhi correction

The classic rule: if the repair exceeds 50% of replacement cost, replace. It is a fair starting point, but it ignores age, so correct it: multiply the unit’s age by the repair cost; if the product exceeds the price of a new unit, replace. A ₹12,000 compressor on a 4-year-old unit scores 48,000 against a ₹45,000 replacement — borderline, and the manufacturer warranty may decide it. The same repair on a 9-year-old unit scores 108,000 — replace, no further discussion needed.

The efficiency term the rules ignore

An old AC does not merely risk failure; it burns money hourly. A tired 10-year-old unit — early-generation efficiency, fixed-speed compressor, a decade of coil wear — can consume 30–50% more energy per hour of cooling than a current inverter 5-star machine. On Delhi duty (8 hours a day, half the year), that difference is commonly worth ₹4,000–₹8,000 a season for a single split at commercial tariffs. Fold that into the comparison: a new unit part-finances itself, and the payback is fastest on exactly the units that run the most.

The refrigerant question

If the unit runs R22 — most Indian ACs installed before roughly 2015 — the decision tilts hard toward replacement for any major repair. R22 is being phased out under India’s HCFC phase-out schedule; the gas is increasingly expensive and scarce, and a compressor-scale repair on an R22 system is new money invested in a dead platform. R410A and R32 systems do not carry this penalty.

Repair it: the cases where the internet is wrong

  • Small parts on healthy machines. Capacitors, relays, fan motors, sensor faults, drain issues — these are maintenance items, not verdicts, at any age where the core (compressor, coils) is sound. A well-maintained 8-year-old unit with a ₹900 capacitor failure has years left in it.
  • A first gas leak on a good unit. Found, repaired, pressure-tested and recharged, a leak is a fixed fault — not the beginning of an annual ritual. (Recurring top-ups without a repair are a different story: that is a leak being farmed, not fixed.)
  • Units under about 5 years. Almost any single repair beats replacement — and check the compressor warranty before paying for one: many brands carry 5–10 year compressor warranties that owners forget they have.

Replace it: the clear cases

  • Compressor failure past year 8–9 — the age-weighted rule almost always says so, and the efficiency term says it louder.
  • The second major repair in two seasons. Cascading failures are a machine’s way of itemising its own obituary. Stop paying instalments on it.
  • Any major repair on an R22 unit.
  • Rust-through of coils or cabinet. Delhi’s monsoon plus pollution corrodes; once coils weep from corrosion, repairs are palliative.
  • The unit was always wrong. Undersized for the room it serves, or so noisy or badly placed that nobody liked it healthy — failure is an exit, take it.

Three worked cases

Case 1: 4-year-old 1.5-ton inverter, PCB failure, ₹6,000 quote

Age × repair = 24,000, well under a ₹45,000 replacement. The unit’s efficiency is current-generation, the compressor may still be in warranty, and the platform (R32) has a long future. Repair — and check whether the brand’s warranty covers the board before paying at all.

Case 2: 9-year-old 1.5-ton fixed-speed, compressor seized, ₹14,000 quote

Age × repair = 126,000 against a ₹45,000 replacement — nearly three times over the line. Add the efficiency term (a current inverter unit saving ₹4,000–₹8,000 a season on this duty) and the repair is not merely uneconomic, it is buying nine-year-old technology at new-part prices. Replace.

Case 3: 11-year-old 2-ton in a shop, second gas leak in 18 months plus a fan motor, ₹7,500 all-in

The age-weighted rule says replace (82,500). But the trend says it louder: recurring leaks on an ageing coil are corrosion announcing itself, and this is the second major visit in two seasons — the machine is itemising its own obituary. The commercial angle seals it: a shop’s cooling failure costs sales, and this unit has become a repeat offender. Replace, before June rather than during it.

Ageing out before failing: the pre-verdict signs

Machines rarely go from healthy to dead; they announce retirement if anyone is recording the announcements. Signs that a unit is entering its last seasons even without a breakdown: current draw creeping up year-on-year for the same duty; longer and longer runs to hold the same set point; a compressor that has become audibly labored on hot afternoons; corrosion blooming on coil ends and the outdoor cabinet; and repair frequency shifting from “every few years” to “every season.” On a contract estate these trends are exactly what per-unit condition histories capture — which is how replacement becomes a budgeted January decision instead of a June emergency. A unit replaced one season early costs you a few months of remaining life; one replaced a season late costs an emergency, a hot week, and whatever the failure took down with it.

Timing and the remains

If the arithmetic says replace, the calendar says do it between October and February: installers are unhurried, and you are not buying in the same week as the rest of the city (see the Delhi calendar). The old unit is not worthless either — scrap and exchange value on a dead split commonly runs ₹1,000–₹3,000, and exchange offers during sale seasons can beat plain scrap. What the old unit should never become is a “spare”: a failed AC stored on a balcony is ₹2,000 of scrap value depreciating with sentiment attached.

What an honest contractor should tell you

The repair-or-replace call is where you learn most about who you are dealing with, because the incentives are misaligned in both directions: a repair shop earns from the repair, a dealer earns from the replacement. The protection is the same in both cases — a diagnosis with readings you can see, both options priced in writing, and the arithmetic above done in front of you. That is our standard on every repair call: quote first, and if the honest quote is “don’t fix this one,” you hear it before money moves.

For fleets: make it a policy, not an argument

Facility managers face this decision per unit, per season, and reinventing it each time invites lobbying. Better: set the thresholds as policy (age-weighted rule; automatic replacement review for R22 plant and second major failures), and drive them from data — per-unit condition grades and trend readings, which is precisely what contract condition reports accumulate. The baseline assessment exists to give an estate that starting ledger: which units are sound, which are watch-list, which are already dead and still drawing salary.
